The Navassa Island Problem

Navassa Island (La Navase) sits ~55 km west of Haiti's Tiburon Peninsula. The sovereignty dispute between Haiti and the United States has cascading effects:

  • US Claim: Guano Islands Act (1856); administered by US Fish & Wildlife Service
  • Haiti Claim: Treaty of Ryswick (1697); Haitian Constitution claims Navassa
  • Impact: Prevents delimitation of Haiti-Jamaica maritime boundary; reduces Haiti's effective EEZ to the west
  • EEZ Implication: If Navassa were recognized as Haitian, Haiti's EEZ could expand by an estimated 10,000-15,000 km2 to the west/southwest
  • Strategic Note: Navassa generates an overlapping claim zone (Marine Regions MRGID 48951) involving US, Haiti, and Jamaica

Haiti has the smallest EEZ per capita of any Caribbean nation and one of the smallest in the world among island states. Despite sharing Hispaniola with the Dominican Republic, Haiti's EEZ is roughly HALF the DR's (126,760 vs 263,598 km2), largely because:

  • The DR has coastline on both the Atlantic and Caribbean, while Haiti's northern coast faces the Windward Passage (shared with Cuba)
  • The Navassa Island dispute shrinks Haiti's western EEZ claim
  • Unresolved boundaries with Jamaica, Bahamas, TCI, and DR compress Haiti's effective zone
  • Haiti's two peninsulas create a deep bight (Golfe de la Gonave) that is largely internal waters, not EEZ

The EEZ Compression Effect

Haiti's 126,760 km2 EEZ is constrained on ALL sides:

  • North: Cuba (Windward Passage, equidistant line agreed 1977)
  • West: United States (Navassa dispute)
  • Southwest: Jamaica (unresolved, blocked by Navassa)
  • South/Southwest: Colombia (agreed 1978, distant)
  • East: Dominican Republic (no maritime boundary)
  • Northeast: Turks & Caicos/Bahamas (no agreement)

This means Haiti's theoretical 200 NM zone is compressed to an effective radius much smaller than 200 NM in most directions.

PART VII: EEDTM INTEGRATION

Haiti's Maritime Extraction Theta

The maritime domain represents another vector of extraction from Haiti:

Current State:

  • Haiti's EEZ generates ~$0.30/km2 in revenue
  • Caribbean average (Jamaica/Bahamas weighted) is ~$1,000+/km2
  • Haiti captures approximately 0.03% of the value its Caribbean peers extract from equivalent maritime space

IUU Fishing as Extraction:

  • Foreign vessels (primarily from neighboring countries) fish Haiti's EEZ without licenses
  • IUU fishing globally captures 20-50% of total catch in poorly governed waters
  • Estimated IUU catch in Haiti's EEZ: 5,000-15,000 tonnes/year
  • Value of IUU extraction: $10-50M/year taken from Haiti's waters without compensation

The Maritime Theta:

If we model total EEZ value (fish + minerals + services) and compare what Haiti captures vs what is extracted:

  • Total value in Haiti's EEZ (caught/extracted): ~$50-100M/year (including IUU)
  • Haiti's government capture: ~$0-2M/year (no licensing, no enforcement)
  • Maritime Theta: ~0.96-0.98 (Haiti captures 2-4% of its own maritime value; 96-98% is extracted by foreign actors or simply forfeited)

The Navassa Extraction

The US claim on Navassa Island is itself an act of maritime extraction:

  • Navassa generates a potential 10,000-15,000 km2 of EEZ for its controller
  • At Caribbean average revenue rates, this is worth $10-15M/year
  • The US has controlled this since 1857 under the Guano Islands Act (a colonial-era resource extraction statute)
  • Haiti has claimed Navassa since independence (1804) and through its Constitution

Continental Shelf: A Forfeited Asset

Haiti's failure to file a CLCS submission by the 2006 deadline means it has potentially forfeited extended continental shelf rights. While the legal effect of missing the deadline is debated, Haiti's failure to even file preliminary information suggests either:

  • No institutional capacity to conduct the required geological survey
  • No awareness of the deadline
  • No political will to assert maritime claims

This is consistent with the broader EEDTM pattern: extraction-weakened states lose the institutional capacity to assert their own rights, creating a self-reinforcing cycle.

Why Bolivia's Flag Is a Red Flag

  • No coastline = no inspections. Bolivia cannot physically inspect vessels flying its flag. It has no ports, no coast guard presence, no maritime infrastructure. Flag state obligations under SOLAS, MARPOL, and STCW are effectively unenforceable.
  • Minimal fleet, minimal oversight. With ~42 registered vessels globally, the registry generates negligible revenue and has no institutional capacity for compliance monitoring.
  • Recognized by obscure classification societies. DromonClass (Greece) and Sing-Lloyd are not among the major IACS classification societies. These are the societies that attract registries other societies won't touch.
  • The "last resort" flag question. Bolivia's registry attracts vessels that need a flag but may not qualify with more regulated registries. A Bolivia-flagged vessel spending 10,488 hours in Haiti's EEZ raises the immediate question: what legitimate commercial purpose brings a Bolivia-registered ship to waters off a country with which Bolivia has no significant trade relationship?

Known Incidents

No specific Bolivia-flagged vessel incidents documented in Caribbean enforcement databases. This is itself significant... it suggests either (a) the vessels go unintercepted, or (b) the flag is used for activities that don't trigger standard maritime interdiction (e.g., fishing, anchoring/loitering rather than port calls).

  • US State Department intervention. There have been direct meetings between IRB, the Togo government, and the US Department of State "over the registry's failings, with several ships discovered trafficking drugs."
  • "Embarrassing" violations. Lloyd's List reported "Togo turmoil over flag registry status after 'embarrassing' violations."
  • Provisional certificate abuse. Togo issues provisional certificates of registry with minimal due diligence. Vessels obtain provisional registration, operate under Togo's flag, then "hop" to another flag before the provisional period expires... a technique specifically designed to avoid accountability.
  • End-of-life flag. Togo is "excessively popular for end-of-life vessels broken in beaching facilities"... vessels at the end of their operational life that no legitimate registry will touch.
  • Numerous vessel losses. Multiple deaths of ships flying the flag of Togo due to storms, reflecting the poor condition of vessels attracted to this flag.

The "Graveyard" Concept

Certain flags serve as the "last stop" for vessels that cannot register anywhere else:

  • During operational life: Vessels cycle through progressively lower-tier flags as their condition deteriorates
  • At end of life: Flags like Togo, Tanzania, SVG, Comoros, and Palau are "excessively popular for end-of-life vessels broken in beaching facilities"
  • For illicit operations: The same flags that attract end-of-life vessels attract illicit operators, because both seek the same thing... minimal scrutiny

9. JURISDICTION ANALYSIS

Who Has Jurisdiction Over Opacity-Flagged Vessels in Haiti's EEZ?

Under UNCLOS:

ZonePrimary JurisdictionHaiti's Rights
Territorial Sea (0-12 nm)HaitiFull sovereignty, can board/inspect/arrest
Contiguous Zone (12-24 nm)Haiti (limited)Can "prevent" customs/drug offenses
EEZ (24-200 nm)Flag StateEconomic rights only (fishing, resources); cannot board foreign vessels for criminal matters without flag state consent
High SeasFlag State exclusivelyNo Haiti jurisdiction

The Enforcement Gap

This is the critical legal gap that opacity flags exploit:

  • In the EEZ: Haiti has economic jurisdiction (fishing, resource extraction) but NOT criminal jurisdiction over foreign-flagged vessels. To board a Bolivia-flagged vessel in Haiti's EEZ for suspected drug trafficking, Haiti would need to:
  • Contact Bolivia's maritime authority (which has no coast guard, no maritime enforcement capacity)
  • Obtain Bolivia's consent to board
  • Or invoke a bilateral agreement (none exists between Haiti and Bolivia)
  • Flag state responsibility is theoretical. Under UNCLOS, the flag state is responsible for ensuring compliance. But Bolivia has no capacity to enforce anything. Togo's registry is run from Lebanon. Comoros's operator is in Dubai (and EU-sanctioned). The entire system depends on flag states exercising jurisdiction they have no capacity or incentive to exercise.
  • The opacity arbitrage. By registering under a flag state that cannot or will not exercise jurisdiction, vessel operators create a legal vacuum. Haiti cannot act (not their jurisdiction over the vessel). Bolivia/Togo/Comoros will not act (no capacity, no incentive). The vessel operates in a zone of effective impunity.
  • Exception for narcotics. UNCLOS Article 108 requires all states to cooperate in suppression of illicit drug trafficking. Article 110 permits warship boarding on the high seas for suspected drug trafficking. But in the EEZ, even this exception requires flag state cooperation... which opacity flags are designed to frustrate.

  • US occupation (1915-1934)
  • Sporadic NGA updates (post-earthquake 2010 LIDAR, specific port approaches)

That's 7 PDFs for 1,771 km of coastline. The vast majority of Haiti's coast between these points is uncharted at modern standards.

Insight 3: Zero Tide Gauges in 2008

The 2008 report: "It was reported that there is not a single tide gauge to monitor the variations of the sea level in all Haiti."

By 2017, three tide stations existed: Cap Haitian, Carrefour, Les Cayes. But the 2008 report specifically warned: "to install a network is much easier than keeping it working for long." There is no evidence these stations are still operational.

Cross-reference with our data: Without tide gauges, Haiti cannot:

  • Calibrate depth soundings for charts
  • Monitor sea level rise (critical for a country at extreme climate risk)
  • Provide tidal predictions for navigation safety
  • Support any future hydrographic survey work

Insight 4: The University Gap

Both the 2008 and 2017 reports note the same finding: Haiti's university offers zero maritime-related degrees.

2008: "The University in Haiti does not offer any option for its students to follow maritime disciplines, despite the maritime situation of the country."

2017: "There are currently no Maritime Training facilities in Haiti despite Haiti's large Maritime interest within the region."

Cross-reference: This means there is no domestic pipeline for hydrographers, marine scientists, nautical cartographers, maritime lawyers, or blue economy planners. Every expert must be trained abroad (Brazil, France, USA) at enormous per-person cost, then returns to a country with no equipment and no institutional structure to employ their skills.

Insight 5: The Labadee Anomaly

The 2017 report reveals: "Cruise Ship Ports are managed by special agreement with the Ministry of Tourism. One example discussed was the Labadee Cruise Ship Port which is directly managed by the Ministry of Tourism, without any intervention by the APN or SEMANAH."

Labadee is Royal Caribbean's private beach resort on Haiti's north coast. Royal Caribbean conducts its own hydrographic surveys for its cruise ships to dock safely. These surveys are not shared with SEMANAH or NGA. The 2017 IHO team specifically recommended that "guidelines on hydrographic survey specifications may be disseminated to all Haitian administrations and Ministries to be able to collect and safeguard maritime geospatial data acquired according to the IHO Standards."

Cross-reference with our data: This is a micro-extraction pattern. A private corporation surveys Haitian waters for its own profit. The data is proprietary. Haiti gains no institutional knowledge from the survey. The pattern: foreign actors possess more knowledge about Haitian territory than Haiti does.

Insight 7: The CNIGS Parallel... And Why It Matters

Both reports highlight CNIGS (National Geospatial Information Center) as a functioning institution with GIS capability, trained staff, and a legal framework. The 2008 report recommended nautical chart production be assigned to CNIGS. The 2017 report noted CNIGS has complete LIDAR coverage of Haiti's land area.

The asymmetry: Haiti has mapped its LAND (via CNIGS). It has NOT mapped its SEA (no SHOH). CNIGS was established in 2006 with clear legislation, funding, and a mandate. SHOH was proposed in 2008 and has never been established.

Why? Plausible hypothesis: land mapping was donor-funded for disaster response (earthquake, hurricane preparedness). Sea mapping has no equivalent donor champion. The World Bank, USAID, and EU fund land-based GIS for DRR (Disaster Risk Reduction). Nobody funds maritime GIS because the use case (revenue capture, fisheries management, boundary delimitation) requires a functioning state... which is exactly what extraction prevents.

3. AIS (AUTOMATIC IDENTIFICATION SYSTEM) SATELLITE TRACKING

The AIS Problem for Haiti

AIS compliance for Haiti-relevant vessels is extremely low. Here is why:

  • SOLAS requires AIS only for vessels >300 gross tonnage (international) or >500 GT (domestic)
  • Small fishing boats, yoles, go-fast boats, and local cargo vessels in Haiti are not required to carry AIS
  • Even vessels that have AIS can (and do) turn it off... "going dark"
  • Estimated AIS coverage for Haiti-bound small vessel traffic: <5-10%
  • AIS is useful for tracking legitimate commercial shipping and identifying which large vessels deviate from normal patterns, but it will NOT detect the small-boat trafficking that is the primary threat
