Part I

The Charcoal Crisis

Haiti's energy system is burning itself alive

Haiti consumes approximately 4 million metric tons of wood for charcoal production every year. Over 90% of Haitian households rely on charcoal as their primary cooking fuel. This is not a cultural preference. It is the absence of alternatives. There is no national gas grid, no reliable electricity outside narrow commercial corridors, and no distribution infrastructure for LPG beyond a handful of urban depots. Charcoal is Haiti's energy system.

The result has been the most extreme deforestation in the Western Hemisphere.

This is not a preference problem. There is no alternative fuel infrastructure. No LPG distribution network reaches rural Haiti. Solar cookstoves exist in pilot quantities but cannot replace charcoal at scale. Until an alternative feedstock can produce charcoal of equivalent heat, burn time, and price... the trees will keep falling.

Prior Studies

This study builds directly on the work of Melinda Miles, who conducted two national charcoal studies for Haiti (2013, 2017). Her research documented the full supply chain from forest to market, quantified production volumes by region, and identified the structural barriers to adoption of improved cookstoves and alternative fuels. Miles' key finding... that charcoal demand is structurally inelastic and supply-side intervention is the only viable path... is the foundational premise of this comparative study.

We are not asking Haitians to stop using charcoal. We are asking whether charcoal can be made from something other than trees.

"The charcoal value chain is the single largest employer in rural Haiti, supporting an estimated 150,000 to 200,000 producers and 50,000 to 100,000 vendors. Any intervention that ignores this labor force will fail."
— Adapted from Miles, National Charcoal Study (2017)

Key insight: Elephant grass optimizes for speed and throughput. Bamboo optimizes for durability and secondary value. Traditional wood optimizes for nothing... it is simply the default in the absence of alternatives. The real answer is likely both alternative feedstocks deployed at different sites based on local conditions, soil quality, and secondary use needs.

Elephant Grass (Napier Grass, Pennisetum purpureum)

BonZeb (proposed franchise partner, not yet confirmed) has developed improved kiln technology specifically designed for high-moisture biomass like elephant grass. Napier grass is among the fastest-growing plants on Earth, reaching 3-4 meters in 90 days under tropical conditions. It is already present in Haiti, used as animal fodder and property boundary markers. As a charcoal feedstock, it offers near-continuous harvest potential... a single planting can yield 4-6 cuts per year for 5-8 years before replanting is needed.

The primary unknown is consumer acceptance. Elephant grass charcoal burns differently than wood charcoal. It may produce less heat per unit mass, and the briquette form factor differs from the irregular chunks consumers are accustomed to. These are testable hypotheses.

Bamboo (Bambusa vulgaris)

Jupille / BambouFacile is already a partner, providing bamboo for perimeter phytoremediation at SAKALA franchise sites. Bambusa vulgaris is the most common bamboo species in Haiti and throughout the Caribbean. It is extraordinarily hardy, grows on marginal land, tolerates poor soil, and its root systems actively remediate heavy-metal-contaminated ground.

Bamboo charcoal has been extensively studied in Asia. Japanese binchotan (white charcoal from bamboo or oak) commands premium prices for its purity, long burn time, and low ash content. The question is not whether bamboo makes good charcoal... it does. The question is whether Bambusa vulgaris, processed through BonZeb-style kilns in Haiti, can be produced at a price point competitive with traditional wood charcoal. That requires this study.

Bamboo's secondary value proposition is substantial: construction material, furniture stock, fencing, erosion barriers, and documented phytoremediation of lead, cadmium, and zinc from contaminated industrial soils. At SAKALA franchise sites located on remediation-target land, the bamboo perimeter is already there. Converting thinning harvests to charcoal feedstock is a zero-marginal-cost input.

Consumer Preference Protocol

Blind comparison test design: 50 households receive three unmarked charcoal samples (A, B, C). Each household cooks the same standardized meal (rice and beans) with each sample on separate days. They rate each on five criteria: (1) ease of lighting, (2) heat intensity, (3) burn duration, (4) smoke level, (5) food taste/smell. Ratings on 1-5 Likert scale. Order randomized across households. Surveyor records time-to-boil with each sample as an objective complement to subjective ratings.

Comparative Advantage Analysis

The elephant grass advantage: Faster growth, more harvests per year, quicker break-even. The feedstock is essentially continuous. A single hectare can yield 40-80 tons of dry biomass per year under optimal conditions. For pure charcoal volume, elephant grass wins.
The bamboo advantage: Dual-use value (construction + charcoal), phytoremediation of contaminated soil, higher BTU potential, longer burn time (based on Asian comparators). At SAKALA franchise sites where bamboo is already planted for perimeter remediation, feedstock cost approaches zero... the thinning harvest is a waste product turned into revenue.
The real answer: Deploy BOTH. Different feedstocks for different sites based on local conditions. Elephant grass for sites with available cultivated land and high charcoal demand. Bamboo for sites on contaminated or marginal land where phytoremediation is needed and secondary construction materials add value. The franchise model supports site-level customization.

Part VIII

The Bottom Line

Three numbers. One question.
4M tons Wood Burned / Year
2% Forest Remaining
2 Renewable Alternatives

Haiti burns 4 million tons of wood for charcoal every year. Two percent of forest cover remains. Two renewable feedstocks... elephant grass and bamboo... can produce equivalent charcoal with zero deforestation. Neither feedstock is theoretical. Both are already growing in Haiti. Both have been carbonized successfully in other contexts. What is missing is a rigorous, Haiti-specific comparative study that measures performance, cost, and consumer acceptance side by side.

That is what this study provides.

The question is not whether Haiti can afford to switch. The question is whether Haiti can afford not to.

The study costs $25,000-40,000. It employs 5 youth researchers for 6 months. It produces 2-3 publishable papers. It generates the baseline data required for carbon credit certification. And it answers, with data rather than assumptions, whether green charcoal can compete on the only metric that matters to a Haitian household: does it cook the food, and can I afford it.

Every month this study does not happen, Haiti loses another few hundred hectares of forest to charcoal production. Every year of delay is another ~4 million tons of wood gone. The math is simple. The solution is testable. The franchise model is ready.

What we need to begin:

1. BonZeb partnership confirmation ... kiln technology and elephant grass cultivation guidance
2. $25-40K in study funding ... from CCA, GEF, academic grants, or a combination
3. Lab partner ... UWI SALISES or equivalent for bomb calorimetry and soil analysis
4. 6 months ... from first batch to submitted paper

The infrastructure exists. The partners are identified. The protocols are designed. The youth researchers are ready.

Let's burn grass instead of trees.
