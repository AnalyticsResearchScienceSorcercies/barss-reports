## 5B. Jerky Processing: Exact Steps

**Equipment Needed:**
- Sharp boning knife + cleaver/meat shears
- Cutting board
- Food dehydrator (Excalibur recommended, $150-300) OR oven
- Vacuum sealer (FoodSaver ~$50-100, Excalibur EHVR12 $179.99)
- Vacuum bags
- Oxygen absorbers (100-count ~$8-15)
- Marinade containers (non-reactive)
- Meat slicer (optional, $30-100) or freeze meat 1-2 hours for easier slicing

**Step-by-Step Process:**

1. **Preparation:** Trim ALL fat and sinew from rabbit meat (rabbit is very lean, so minimal trimming needed). Cut meat into thin strips, 1/4 inch thick, cutting WITH the grain for chewy jerky or AGAINST the grain for tender jerky.

2. **Marinade:** Combine soy sauce, Worcestershire sauce, brown sugar or honey, garlic powder, onion powder, smoked paprika, black pepper. For Haitian twist: add epis (garlic, scotch bonnet, thyme, parsley, lime juice, clove).

3. **Marinate:** Seal strips in bag or covered container. Refrigerate minimum 4 hours, ideally overnight (12 hours). Rabbit absorbs flavors quickly due to lean composition.

4. **Dehydrate:** Preheat dehydrator to 160 degrees F (71 degrees C). Lay strips on trays without overlapping, ensure airflow. Dehydrate 4-6 hours.

5. **Doneness Test:** Bend a piece gently. Should bend without snapping. Leathery texture, no visible moisture inside. Should NOT be brittle.

6. **Condition:** Let cool completely. Place in ziplock bag loosely for 24 hours at room temperature. Check for any moisture spots. If moisture appears, dehydrate longer.

7. **Package:** Vacuum seal in food-grade bags with oxygen absorber. Label with production date.

## 5C. FDA/USDA Requirements for Commercial Rabbit Jerky

**CRITICAL REGULATORY ADVANTAGE: Rabbits are classified as "NOT AMENABLE" to USDA/FSIS inspection.**

This means:
- USDA meat inspection is NOT required for rabbit
- Rabbit falls under FDA jurisdiction (not USDA/FSIS)
- You do NOT need a USDA-inspected facility to process rabbit
- However, you DO need to comply with FDA food safety regulations
- For interstate sale, you need proper food facility registration with FDA
- HACCP plan still recommended/required depending on state
- State-level regulations vary... check specific state requirements

**For USDA Voluntary Inspection:**
- Available but you must pay for it
- Gives you the USDA inspection mark (marketing advantage)
- Required if selling to certain institutional buyers

**Labeling Requirements (FDA for non-amenable species):**
- Product name
- Net weight
- Ingredient list (descending order by weight)
- Nutrition Facts panel
- Allergen declarations
- Name and address of manufacturer/distributor
- "Keep Refrigerated" or storage instructions as applicable
- If water activity 0.85-0.91: must state "Refrigerate After Opening"
- If water activity below 0.85: truly shelf-stable, no refrigeration needed

**Water Activity Standards for Shelf Stability:**
- Must be below 0.85 aw for shelf-stable without refrigeration statement
- Must be below 0.88 aw for oxygen-free packaging
- Moisture-to-Protein Ratio (MPR): 0.75:1 or less for "jerky" classification

## 5D. Shelf Life

- **Vacuum sealed with oxygen absorber:** 1-2 years (store-bought commercial standard is ~2 years unopened)
- **Vacuum sealed without oxygen absorber:** 3-6 months
- **Sealed in ziplock/bag without vacuum:** 1-2 months at room temperature
- **After opening:** 1-2 weeks (store in cool, dry place)
- **Oxygen absorbers reduce O2 to <0.01%**, stopping bacterial/mold growth
- **No specific rabbit jerky shelf-life study exists** -- apply beef jerky standards (same dehydration physics apply to lean meats)

## 5E. Packaging Requirements

- **Vacuum seal bags:** Must be food-grade, high-barrier material (blocks oxygen, moisture, light)
- **Oxygen absorbers:** 50-100cc per bag (depends on bag size and headspace)
- **Labeling:** Must include all FDA-required elements listed above
- **Packaging material:** Foil-lined stand-up pouches preferred for retail (light barrier)
- **Batch coding/lot numbers:** Required for recall traceability

# SECTION 6: FEED -- MORINGA INTEGRATION

## 6A. Moringa Leaf Meal in Rabbit Diets: The Research

**CRITICAL FINDING: Moringa is an EXCELLENT rabbit feed, validated by multiple peer-reviewed studies.**

### Key Studies and Results:

**Study 1:** Ayssiwede et al. (2011) -- Published in *Tropical Animal Health and Production*
- Leucaena and Moringa leaf meal compared
- Moringa at 30% inclusion: highest final body weight (2,040g for control vs 2,000g for 30% MOLM)
- Daily weight gain: 31.6 g/day (control) vs 30.6 g/day (30% MOLM) -- statistically equivalent
- Feed conversion ratio: 3.4 (control) vs 3.2 (30% MOLM) -- MORINGA WAS BETTER
- 30% and 40% MOLM improved economic efficiency (2.0 and 1.5 vs 1.1 for control)

**Study 2:** Abu Ahammad et al. (2017) -- *PubMed 28603877*
- Moringa as substitute for alfalfa meal
- 20% moringa substitution (MOL20): significantly better daily weight gain and FCR than other groups
- No adverse effects on nutrient digestibility, carcass traits, or meat quality
- Improved antioxidant capacity and biochemical parameters

**Study 3:** Manyeula et al. (2025) -- Systematic Review & Meta-Analysis, *Food Science & Nutrition*
- Meta-analysis across multiple studies
- Rabbits fed MOLM-based diets: similar feed intake, higher average daily gain, improved FCR
- Improved red blood cell count, hemoglobin, white blood cell count
- Decreased malondialdehyde (oxidative stress marker), increased glutathione (antioxidant)

**Study 4:** Nigerian Journal of Animal Science
- Graded levels of MOLM tested
- MOLM can be included up to 40% without adverse effects on growth or carcass traits
- 15% MOLM can replace soybean meal entirely

**Study 5:** Meat Science (2023) -- ScienceDirect
- Moringa leaves as sole feed or combined with concentrate
- 70% MOLM inclusion: beneficial for improved growth performance AND functional attributes of meat
- Meat quality attributes improved (antioxidant properties in the meat itself)

### Optimal Inclusion Levels Summary:

| Level | Effect | Source |
|-------|--------|--------|
| 5% | Supplement/health booster | General recommendation |
| 15% | Can fully replace soybean meal | Nigerian study |
| 20% | Best FCR when replacing alfalfa | Abu Ahammad 2017 |
| 25-30% | Optimal balance of growth + economics | Multiple studies |
| 30-40% | No adverse effects, improved economics | Ayssiwede 2011 |
| 70% | Still beneficial, improved meat quality | Meat Science 2023 |
| 95% | Tested with positive results in pellet form | Research literature |

**RECOMMENDATION FOR HAITI FRANCHISE: 25-30% moringa leaf meal in feed mix. This is the sweet spot for cost savings (moringa grows FREE in Haiti), growth performance, and meat quality.**

## 6B. Other Local Haiti Feeds

### Sweet Potato Vines (Ipomoea batatas)

- Can replace 25-75% of commercial feed components
- Tested at 25%, 50%, 75%, 100% replacement levels in New Zealand White rabbits
- Widely available in Haiti
- Sweet potato foliage supports good performance
- **Recommended inclusion: 15-25% of diet**

### Banana Leaves/Peels
- Less nutritious than other forages
- Used more as famine/substitute feed
- Banana PEELS have better nutritional value than leaves
- Can be part of a forage mix but should not dominate
- **Recommended inclusion: 5-10% maximum**

### Leucaena (Leucaena leucocephala)
- Contains mimosine (toxic amino acid) -- CAUTION
- Safe only in small quantities: maximum 25% of ration (1/4)
- Unsatisfactory as sole forage
- Useful as COMPONENT of forage mixture
- Available in Haiti (nitrogen-fixing tree, common in tropical agroforestry)
- **Recommended inclusion: 10-15% maximum, never as sole forage**

### Lalo/Jute Mallow (Corchorus olitorius)
- **YES, rabbits can eat mallow family plants.** Mallow (Malva spp.) is documented as safe for rabbits -- all parts.
- Jute mallow (Corchorus olitorius) nutritional profile: 12.5-29% crude protein (varies by study), high in calcium, iron, folate, vitamin C
- No specific rabbit feeding trial found for C. olitorius, but the mallow family is broadly safe
- **Caution:** Some mallows can be toxic to sheep/horses in high nitrogen conditions, but no documented rabbit toxicity
- **CIRCULAR ECONOMY LOOP CONFIRMED:** Lalo is a traditional Haitian food crop. Leaves/trimmings that don't meet market grade can feed rabbits. Rabbit manure feeds lalo. Lalo feeds people AND rabbits.
- **Recommended inclusion: 5-15% as forage component, introduce gradually**

### Guinea Grass / Local Grasses
- Standard base forage for tropical rabbit operations
- The Hares for Haiti program trains farmers to gather locally grown grasses
- **Recommended inclusion: 30-40% of diet as base forage**

# SECTION 7: DISEASE IN HAITI/CARIBBEAN

## 7A. Documented Rabbit Diseases

**Coccidiosis** -- THE primary rabbit disease concern in tropical environments
- Caused by Eimeria protozoa (multiple species)
- Two forms: hepatic (liver) and intestinal
- Common and worldwide, especially prevalent in humid tropical conditions
- Treatment: Toltrazuril (10 mg/kg daily for 3 days, repeat after 3 days)
- Prevention: Sulfaquinoxaline in drinking water (0.04% for 30 days)
- Control, not cure, is the realistic goal

**Pasteurellosis** (Snuffles)
- Caused by Pasteurella multocida bacteria
- Respiratory infections, abscesses, chronic inflammation
- Spread through direct contact and respiratory droplets
- Treatment: Antibiotics (enrofloxacin, trimethoprim-sulfa)

**Parasites (External)**
- Ear mites, fur mites (Cheyletiella)
- Treatment: Ivermectin 200-400 mcg/kg subcutaneous, 2-3 treatments 10-21 days apart
- Alternative: Doramectin 0.5 mg/kg

**Parasites (Internal)**
- Nematodes (roundworms)
- Treatment: Ivermectin 0.3-0.5 mg/kg subcutaneous, repeat after 7-14 days

**NO documented myxomatosis or RHD outbreaks in Haiti/Caribbean.** These are primarily European/Australian diseases. Myxomatosis vaccine is not even available in the US. RHD is an emerging concern in North America but has not been documented in Haiti.

## 7B. Veterinary Services for Rabbits in Haiti

- **FAMV** (Faculte d'Agronomie et de Medecine Veterinaire) -- State University of Haiti, founded 1924. Trains veterinarians and agronomists. No specific rabbit program but graduates provide general livestock veterinary services.
- **VETERIMED** -- Primary livestock health NGO, supports 700,000+ small farmer families. Rabbit health would fall under their mandate.
- **Private veterinarians:** Limited availability, concentrated in Port-au-Prince
- **F2F volunteers** like Dr. Myriam Kaplan-Pasternak (DVM) provide periodic training

## 7C. Deworming Products Available in Haiti

- **Ivermectin:** Widely available in Haiti for livestock use (cattle/goat formulations can be used for rabbits at adjusted dosage). Available at agricultural supply stores.
- **Toltrazuril:** Less commonly available; may need to import. This is the gold standard for coccidiosis.
- **Sulfaquinoxaline:** Available as poultry medication, applicable to rabbits in drinking water.
- **Brand names vary** by what is imported; generic veterinary ivermectin is most accessible.
- **Prices:** Ivermectin injectable (50ml bottle) typically $5-10 USD in Haitian agricultural supply shops.

## 7D. Vaccination Protocols

**For Haiti operations: NO VACCINATION IS NEEDED.**

- Myxomatosis: Not present in the Americas. Vaccine not available in US/Haiti.
- RHD: Not documented in Haiti. Vaccine availability limited even in US.
- The combined Myxo-RHD PLUS vaccine is a European product (Nobivac).
- Focus should be on SANITATION and MANAGEMENT, not vaccination:
  - Clean cages daily
  - Wire mesh floors (droppings fall through)
  - Quarantine new animals
  - Separate sick animals immediately
  - Proper ventilation
  - Clean water supply

## 8D. Rabbit Skin/Fur Market

**Raw Pelts:**
- Untanned fryer pelts: $2-3 each
- Tanned pelts: $4-6 each (eBay/online)
- Craft grade (minor imperfections): $2.75 each
- Bulk tanned (6-pack on Amazon): ~$3-4 each

**Tanning Cost:**
- Professional tanning: $10-16 per hide (minimum 75 hides for $10 rate)
- Home tanning (alum + salt method): ~$0.50-1.00 per hide in materials

**Home Tanning Process (Alum Method):**
1. Mix 2 gallons lukewarm water + 1 cup non-iodized salt + 1 cup alum
2. Soak hides 2-7 days, stir twice daily
3. Add another cup each of salt and alum
4. Soak at least 1 more week, stir twice daily
5. Test: cut small piece, boil. If it curls/hardens, not ready
6. When done, coat backside with mink oil or neatsfoot oil

**Market in Haiti:** No established rabbit skin market. Potential for:
- Artisan leather goods (wallets, phone cases, book covers)
- Craft supplies for local artisans
- Export to US craft market via SAKALA/TapTap channels
- Pet toy market (significant US demand for rabbit skin dog toys)

**Realistic Revenue per Skin in Haiti Context:** $1-2 per raw skin sold to an aggregator; $3-5 if home-tanned and sold as craft material.

**KEY ADVANTAGE: Rabbit manure is "COLD" manure.** Can be applied DIRECTLY to gardens/fields without composting, without burning plants. This is unique among animal manures and massively reduces labor for farmers.

- **Estimated value in Haiti:** If sold/traded to neighbors at even $0.05-0.10/lb, that is $2.50-$10/month additional income

# SECTION 9: FEED CONVERSION & PRODUCTION METRICS

## Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR)
- Commercial pellet diet: 2.5-3.0 (best)
- Mixed forage + supplement: 3.5-4.0
- 30% moringa diet: 3.2 (comparable to commercial)
- Tropical forage-heavy diet: 4.0-4.5

## Carcass Yield
- Dressing percentage: 50-56% (tropical conditions)
- A 2.0 kg live rabbit yields ~1.0-1.1 kg carcass
- Meat yield (boneless): ~65-70% of carcass weight
- **Net meat per rabbit: ~650-770g (1.4-1.7 lbs)**

## Growth Rate
- Slaughter weight achieved: 2.0 kg at 12-14 weeks (commercial breeds)
- Local Haitian crossbreed: smaller, may take 14-16 weeks
- New Zealand White in tropics: 2.0-2.5 kg at 12 weeks

## Reproduction
- Gestation: 31 days
- Litter size: 4-12 kits (average 6-8)
- Weaning: 4-5 weeks
- Breeding age: 5-6 months
- Litters per year: 5-6 (with rest periods)
- **One doe can produce 30-48 fryers per year**

## USDA/FDA Regulatory Path for Rabbit Jerky
Since rabbit is NOT AMENABLE to USDA inspection:
1. Register facility with FDA as food establishment
2. Develop HACCP plan
3. Comply with FDA labeling requirements
4. State food manufacturing license (varies by state)
5. Product liability insurance
6. **NO USDA inspector required on-site**

This is SIGNIFICANTLY easier and cheaper than beef/chicken/pork jerky production.
