1. The Problem: Haiti’s Charcoal Death Spiral

Haiti has less than 1% of its primary forest remaining. In 1950, forest cover was 50%. The driver is not mysterious... it is arithmetic. 94% of Haitian households cook with wood charcoal. The country produces 946,500 metric tons of charcoal annually, cutting 53,000 hectares of trees per year while replanting only 2,000. Net loss: 51,000 hectares per year, every year, accelerating.

The numbers that define the crisis:
$392 million — annual charcoal market value, ~5% of GDP
9 million people — dependent on wood charcoal for daily cooking
50%+ of income — spent on cooking fuel by households in Cité Soleil
10,000 bags/day — burned across Port-au-Prince alone
51,000 hectares/year — net deforestation for charcoal production
0 commercial alternatives — currently at market scale

The conventional response to Haiti’s charcoal crisis has been to tell people to stop using charcoal. That has never worked and will never work. Charcoal is not a preference... it is the only affordable cooking fuel available. LPG costs 3–5x more. Solar cookers do not work for traditional Haitian cuisine. Electric stoves require a grid that does not exist.

The solution is not to eliminate charcoal demand. The solution is to change the feedstock... from trees that take 15–30 years to regrow to a grass that grows 4 feet every 4 months and thrives on slopes too steep to farm.

The insight: Haiti does not have a charcoal problem. Haiti has a feedstock problem. The $392M demand is permanent. The question is what you burn... not whether you burn. Elephant grass answers that question in 180 days.

2. The Solution: Elephant Grass on Slopes + BonZeb Kiln

Elephant grass (Cenchrus purpureus, formerly Pennisetum purpureum) is a perennial tropical grass that reaches 3–5 meters in 180 days. It produces 30–40 tonnes of dry matter per hectare per year under standard conditions, and up to 80–100 tonnes under optimal management. It grows on steep hillsides where no food crop can survive. Its dense root system holds soil that would otherwise wash into the sea during every hurricane season.

The critical technology that makes this viable is the BonZeb kiln... a carbonization unit designed specifically for elephant grass by Tom Stein’s team, engineered to be 99% person-powered and 95% smoke-free. No diesel. No electricity. No imported components. A kiln that can be built and operated by a crew of young Haitians on the same hillside where the grass grows.

How It Works: Slope to Stove

4. Charcoal Production: Kiln, Briquetting, Packaging

Stage 1: Harvesting

Cut mature elephant grass (4–12 months old) at 10–15 cm above ground level using machete or mechanical cutter. Leave the root crown and stubble intact... regrowth begins within days. Field-dry the cut stalks for 3–7 days to reduce moisture content below 20%. Optimal moisture at carbonization: 10–15%.

Stage 2: Carbonization (BonZeb Kiln)

BonZeb Kiln Specifications

FeatureSpecification
Power source99% person-powered
Emissions95% smoke-free
Designed forElephant grass specifically (not adapted from wood kiln)
Fuel/electricityNone required

The kiln uses pyrolysis to convert dried grass biomass into charcoal at approximately 30% mass yield. The process is batch-based: load the kiln, seal, ignite, wait for carbonization cycle to complete, cool, unload char.

Stage 3: Briquetting

Why briquetting is non-negotiable: Elephant grass charcoal is POWDERY after carbonization. Unlike wood charcoal, which retains its solid form, grass char crumbles into fine powder. You cannot sell powder to a Haitian cook. You must compress it into briquettes that look, feel, and burn like the charcoal people already know.
Briquetting ParameterSpecification
Binder materialCassava starch (manioc)
Binder ratio4–8% by weight
Starch preparationGelatinized (cooked with water to form paste)
MixingCharcoal powder + starch paste, mixed by hand or mechanical mixer
PressingManual lever press or screw press (~$50–150 for manual unit)
Briquette shapeCylindrical with center hole (pillow, honeycomb, or egg shapes also viable)
Drying time2–5 days sun-drying (humidity dependent)
Final moisture<8% for optimal burn and shelf life
Why cassava starch: Cassava (manioc) is the most widely grown crop in Haiti. Starch extraction is a known skill. The binder is locally sourced, cheap, food-safe, and burns cleanly. No imported petroleum-based binders. No chemicals. A SAKALA franchise can grow its own cassava for binder on the same land or source from neighboring farmers... another revenue link in the cooperative chain.

Stage 4: Packaging & Distribution

Briquettes are packaged in standard-weight bags (5 kg, 10 kg, 25 kg) matching existing charcoal market formats. SAKALA branding. Sold through existing market channels... the same madan sara distribution network that moves wood charcoal today. The product slots into existing infrastructure. No new retail channel needed.

5. Dual Purpose: Livestock Feed + Charcoal from Same Crop

Elephant grass is not a single-use crop. The same planting generates two revenue streams from the same land, depending on when you harvest.

Young Harvest (1–3 Months)

Use: Livestock feed
Protein content: 6–10%
Digestibility: High
Market: Goat, cattle, rabbit feed
Revenue: Sold fresh or dried to livestock operations

At this stage, the grass is tender, leafy, and nutritious. It can be fed directly to livestock or chopped and dried as hay. This is the same grass species that SAKALA rabbit franchises can use as feed supplement.

Mature Harvest (4–12 Months)

Use: Charcoal feedstock
Fiber content: High (lignified)
Digestibility: Low (too woody for feed)
Market: Charcoal briquettes
Revenue: $10,000–15,000/ha/yr net

At maturity, the grass has lignified. It is ideal for carbonization. High fiber content = high charcoal yield. The stems are thick, dry easily, and carbonize efficiently in the BonZeb kiln.

The franchise integration: A SAKALA site can allocate a portion of its elephant grass planting to early-cut feed for its own rabbit operation (see Rabbit Playbook), while the majority grows to maturity for charcoal. Same land, same labor, two products, two revenue streams. The young grass feeds the rabbits. The mature grass feeds the kiln. The rabbit manure fertilizes the grass. Circular.

8. Slope Planting & Erosion Control

Haiti has one of the highest soil erosion rates on earth. Deforested mountainsides lose topsoil with every rainstorm. Hurricane season turns bare slopes into mudslide zones. Port-au-Prince’s informal settlements are built below these slopes. Erosion is not an environmental abstraction... it kills people.

Why Elephant Grass on Slopes Changes Everything

  • Dense root system penetrates deep into hillside soil, binding it in place. Root mass develops within 60–90 days of planting.
  • Steep terrain is an advantage, not a barrier. Elephant grass thrives on slopes too steep for any food crop. These are the exact hillsides currently eroding into nothing.
  • Slope land is cheap or unclaimed. Nobody farms 45-degree mountainsides. SAKALA franchises can access this land at minimal or zero cost.
  • Reduces mudslide risk during hurricanes. Established elephant grass stands slow water runoff, trap sediment, and prevent the cascading slope failures that destroy downhill communities.
  • Contour planting (rows along the contour lines of the slope) creates natural terraces over time as sediment builds up behind each row.
  • No irrigation required. Hillside rainfall capture is sufficient. The grass is drought-tolerant once established.
The strategic logic: Every other SAKALA franchise type (rabbits, lalo, moringa, vetiver) needs flat or gently sloping land. Elephant grass takes the land NOBODY ELSE CAN USE and turns it into the highest-revenue-per-hectare crop in the franchise system. The worse the terrain, the better the case for elephant grass. And the worse the terrain, the cheaper the land.

10. Carbon Credits

Elephant grass charcoal production generates verifiable carbon removal. This is not an abstraction... it is a revenue stream that international markets are already paying for.

Additional: avoided deforestationVariableEach tonne of grass charcoal = trees NOT cut

Three Carbon Revenue Pathways

  • Pathway 1: Biochar carbon removal credits. When charcoal is applied to soil (as biochar) rather than burned, it stores carbon for centuries. The international voluntary carbon market pays $179/tonne CO2 for biochar-based removal... the cheapest form of durable carbon dioxide removal (CDR). 87% of all CDR deliveries globally come from biochar. A SAKALA franchise can divert a portion of charcoal production to biochar for soil amendment and sell the carbon credits.
  • Pathway 2: Avoided deforestation credits. Every tonne of grass charcoal sold replaces a tonne of wood charcoal that would have required cutting trees. This avoided deforestation is creditable under REDD+ and voluntary carbon standards. Haiti’s near-zero forest baseline makes this particularly compelling.
  • Pathway 3: Soil carbon accumulation. Elephant grass root systems deposit organic carbon into soil continuously. Over 5–10 years, slope plantings measurably increase soil organic carbon... generating credits under soil carbon verification programs.
Conservative projection: At 1 tonne CO2e/ha/year and $179/tonne, a 2-hectare franchise unit generates $358/year in carbon revenue from Year 2. This is supplementary income on top of charcoal sales... essentially free money for growing grass on a hillside. At 10+ hectares, carbon credits alone can exceed $1,790/year. As carbon markets mature and Haiti gets verified, this number grows.
