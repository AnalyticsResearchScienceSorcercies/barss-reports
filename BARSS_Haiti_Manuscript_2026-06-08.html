Chapter 1 · Paper 1
The 1838 Decision: Fiscal Strangulation and Ideological Conflict (1825-1838)
1.1 The 1825 Ordinance and the "Double Debt" Mechanism

The financial strangulation of Haiti was initiated not in 1838 but in 1825. On April 17, 1825, King Charles X of France issued a Royal Ordinance recognizing Haiti's independence. This recognition was not a diplomatic negotiation but an ultimatum, delivered by a fleet of fourteen brigs of war. The terms were explicitly coercive: in exchange for recognizing an independence already won by 1804, Haiti was forced to pay an indemnity of 150 million francs, officially to compensate former French plantation owners for "lost property" ... a category that implicitly included their former human chattel.

Quantitative analysis of Haiti's economy at the time reveals the sum was not merely burdensome but an a priori impossibility. The 150 million francs represented approximately 300% of Haiti's total 1825 GDP. François Blancpain calculated the indemnity was "the equivalent of ten years of fiscal revenues." Frédérique Beauvois estimated average government revenues between 1818 and 1824 at a mere 2,581,210 francs... making the 150 million franc indemnity approximately 58 years of total state revenue.

The a priori unpayability of the 1825 indemnity was the trigger for the "double debt." Unable to meet the first installment of 30 million francs, the Haitian government under President Boyer was immediately forced to secure a loan for the exact same amount from a consortium of French banks, including Ternaux & Gandolphe and the bank of Jacques Laffitte. This created two parallel and punitive financial streams: (1) the indemnity itself, owed to the French state, and (2) the high-interest commercial loan owed to French financiers, which was required to pay the first stream. Haiti was thus indebted twice-over for the same sum.

1.2 Quantifying the Burden: The "Fiscal Scissor"

The "double debt" mechanism immediately subjected the Haitian state to a "fiscal scissor." This two-bladed mechanism cut off all possibility of domestic investment by simultaneously collapsing state revenue and imposing a massive, inflexible new expenditure.

Blade 1: Collapsing Revenue. The 1825 Ordinance was imposed at the exact moment Haiti's primary revenue source was collapsing. Archival data on coffee prices shows a catastrophic decline: prices for 100 pounds of coffee fell from 290 francs in 1821, to 140 francs in 1825, and to 85 francs in 1830 ... a 71% decline in nine years. France was the primary destination for Haitian coffee. French officials, bankers, and merchants knew the coffee market was in freefall. Imposing a 150 million franc debt at this specific moment was a perfectly timed economic weapon.

Blade 2: Fixed Extractive Cost. Servicing the debt "swallow[ed] at times as much as half of public expenditure." The Haitian government dedicated 50% of its public revenue to debt service until 1850. By 1900, this figure had risen to 80% of the national budget. "Productive investment became impossible." Education and infrastructure expenditures were the first cuts.

1.3 The Internal French Debate: Commerce vs. Colonial Ideology

The 1838 renegotiation was the critical moment when this mechanism could have been reversed. The resulting internal French debate reveals the core thesis of this paper: the final decision was a victory of ideology over economic rationalism.

The Extraction Faction
Ultra-royalists, colonial ministry officials, ex-planter lobby. Arguments rooted in racial hierarchy and "prestige." Demanded punitive compensation including for the value of enslaved human beings. Used inflated revenue figures (Esmangart: "37 million francs") to justify the unpayable sum.
The Commercial Faction
Bordeaux, Le Havre, Marseille merchants and industrialists. Wanted Haiti as a "lucrative trading relationship." The indemnity was counter-productive: it destabilized their coffee supply. Their primary concern was securing "demi-droit" preferential trade terms.
The Financier Faction
Bankers including Laffitte, whose bank floated the 1825 loan. Needed a Haiti that prioritized debt service. Made a "windfall" from high-interest loans. Held Haitian bonds (documented: Rothschild Archive). Tipped scales against the Commercial Faction.
1.4 The 1838 "Traité d'Amitié": A Failure of Economic Pragmatism

King Louis-Philippe dispatched twelve warships to Port-au-Prince to force a new "agreement." The resulting Traité d'Amitié reduced the remaining indemnity to 60 million francs, which, when added to the 30 million francs already owed to bankers, finalized the total debt at 90 million francs.

"Emmanuel de Las Cases ... denounced the 1825 indemnity as irrational. Thirteen years later, his son, Baron Emmanuel Pons de las Cases, arrived in Port-au-Prince on a French warship to enforce that same irrational debt at gunpoint."

The father knew it was wrong. The son enforced it anyway. This is the failure of economic pragmatism.
