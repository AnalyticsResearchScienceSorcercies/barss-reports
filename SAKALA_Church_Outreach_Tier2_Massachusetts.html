Section 1

Executive Summary

Massachusetts is not Florida. That is the whole point.

In Florida, the Haitian diaspora is spread across dozens of ZIP codes from Miami-Dade to Orlando to Port St. Lucie. The logistics demand home delivery... individual boxes to individual doors, $15/box in shipping, thin margins. Florida is the volume play.

Massachusetts is the density play. The Haitian population here clusters so tightly... Brockton, Dorchester, Mattapan, Randolph, Everett... that a fundamentally different distribution model becomes possible. Instead of shipping 390 individual boxes to 390 addresses, you deliver 10 pallets to 39 churches. Subscribers pick up after Sunday service or on a designated Harvest Day. Shipping cost drops from ~$15/box to ~$3/box. The savings flow back into the product, the franchise, and the church itself.

This is the Coop Pickup Model. It turns every participating church into a Pickup Hub... a physical node in the SAKALA distribution network where community gathers around a box of Haitian-grown goods. The church is not just a worship space anymore. It is the marketplace. The fellowship hall is the distribution center. Harvest Day is the new community event.

Why Massachusetts First for Coop Pickup

Brockton ZIP 02301 has 12,294 Haiti-born residents. That is more Haitians per square mile than anywhere in the United States outside of North Miami. Ten Haitian churches sit within that single ZIP code. You could walk between them. This is not a market you need to create. It is a market that already exists, waiting for a product that respects its density.

Marc Guirand runs Job Power... the vocational training pipeline within SAKALA. He has direct relationships with 39 Massachusetts churches through his slideshow directory. That is not a cold outreach list. That is a warm network. The infrastructure for this rollout already exists in the form of trust, pastoral relationships, and Sunday morning foot traffic.

Section 3

The Coop Pickup Model

The Coop Pickup Model inverts the standard subscription box logistics. Instead of last-mile delivery to 390 individual addresses... the most expensive part of any subscription box operation... we deliver in bulk to 39 fixed locations. Subscribers come to the box. The box does not go to the subscriber.

This is not a novel concept. CSA (Community Supported Agriculture) programs have used church and community center pickups for decades. What is novel is applying it to a Haitian cooperative agricultural franchise operating across international borders.

The Church as Pickup Hub

Step 1: Bulk Delivery (Thursday)

A regional delivery vehicle splits the Boston warehouse pallet into church-sized batches. Each batch contains the exact number of boxes matching that church's subscriber count. Boxes arrive pre-labeled with subscriber names. Delivery window: Thursday morning, coordinated with church volunteer coordinator.

Step 2: Storage (Thursday-Saturday)

Boxes are stored in the church's fellowship hall, basement, or designated room. Moringa powder, castor oil, spices, and coffee are shelf-stable. Fresh produce items (if included seasonally) are packed in insulated liners. Most boxes require no refrigeration for the 48-72 hour storage window.

Step 3: Pickup (Saturday or Sunday)

Subscribers pick up their boxes during designated hours. Two models:

  • Sunday Pickup: After morning service. Boxes staged in fellowship hall. Subscribers sign in (paper or QR code), collect their box, stay for coffee. This is the default.
  • Saturday Harvest Day: Dedicated pickup event, 10 AM - 2 PM. More space, more time, option to add tasting tables, cooking demos, franchise updates. Monthly or quarterly special event.

Step 4: Reconciliation

Volunteer coordinator confirms pickups via simple sign-in sheet or QR scan app. Uncollected boxes held until Tuesday, then donated to church food ministry. Pickup data feeds back to SAKALA operations for forecasting.

The Pastor Pitch (Massachusetts Edition)

The pastor pitch in Massachusetts is different from Florida. In Florida, you are asking a church to recommend a product. In Massachusetts, you are asking a church to become a location. That is a bigger ask. It requires a bigger answer.

The Core Message
"Pastor, your church is already where the community gathers. We are asking you to let the community gather around something that feeds Haiti. Every quarter, a pallet of Haitian-grown goods arrives at your door. Your members pick up their boxes after service. Your church earns $2 per box for hosting, plus 5% on every new subscription you refer. Your fellowship hall becomes a marketplace. Your parking lot becomes a Harvest Day festival. And 310 youth in Haiti get to keep working because your church said yes."

The Five Selling Points (MA-Specific)

1. Your Church Becomes a Community Hub

Church attendance nationwide is declining. Not because faith is declining... because churches compete with convenience. The Coop Pickup Model gives people a reason to come beyond Sunday morning. Harvest Day is a community event. It is a market, a tasting, a conversation, a celebration. It makes your church the place where something happens.

2. Pickup Days Create Foot Traffic and Fellowship

Every subscriber who walks in for their box stays for 10 minutes. Some stay for an hour. Some bring their kids. Some bring a neighbor. That neighbor sees the church, meets the pastor, learns what SAKALA is. Pickup days are organic outreach events. You do not need a marketing budget. You need a folding table and a pot of coffee.

3. Bulk Delivery = Lower Cost = More Value Per Box

By hosting pickup, your congregation saves $12/box compared to home delivery. That savings means bigger boxes, better products, or lower prices for your members. The church is not just hosting... the church is creating economic value for its members by providing a distribution point.

Section 7

Pickup Hub Requirements

A church does not need a loading dock to become a Pickup Hub. It needs four things.

1. Covered Storage Area

Boxes arrive Thursday. Pickup happens Saturday or Sunday. The church needs a dry, indoor space to hold boxes for 48-72 hours. A fellowship hall corner. A Sunday school room. A closet. Space for 10 boxes = approximately 3 feet x 3 feet x 4 feet.

2. One Volunteer Coordinator

One person. Two hours per week. Receives delivery on Thursday (15 min). Stages boxes for pickup (15 min). Manages sign-in during pickup window (60 min). Reports pickup data (15 min). Church designates this person. SAKALA provides training.

3. Pickup Tracking System

Simple. Two options:

  • Paper: Printed checklist of subscriber names. Check off on pickup. Photo and text to SAKALA coordinator.
  • QR Code: Each subscriber has a membership QR. Scan at pickup using volunteer's phone. Auto-reports to SAKALA dashboard.

4. Pickup Window

Minimum 2 hours per week. Recommended:

  • Primary: Sunday after morning service (12:00-2:00 PM)
  • Alternate: Saturday morning (10:00 AM-12:00 PM)
  • Special: Harvest Day quarterly event (full afternoon)

What SAKALA Provides to Each Hub

  • Pre-labeled boxes with subscriber names (no sorting needed)
  • Printed pickup checklist each quarter
  • QR code scanner app (free, runs on any smartphone)
  • Branded table banner for pickup area
  • Flyers and sign-up cards for new subscribers (replenished quarterly)
  • Volunteer coordinator training (one 30-minute video call)
  • Harvest Day event kit (quarterly): tablecloths, tasting cups, recipe cards, franchise update poster
  • $2/box hub fee paid monthly via direct deposit
  • Product liability insurance coverage for stored inventory

What SAKALA Does NOT Require

  • No refrigeration (shelf-stable products)
  • No cash handling (all payments via SAKALA app/website)
  • No sales activity (church is a pickup point, not a retail location)
  • No financial investment from the church
  • No exclusivity (church can host other programs simultaneously)

The Brockton Story

There is a narrative reason to start in Brockton that goes beyond logistics. Brockton is not glamorous. It is not Cambridge or Beacon Hill. It is a working-class city with a Haitian community that built itself from nothing over 40 years. The shoe factories that once powered Brockton are gone. What replaced them, in part, was the Haitian community itself... its churches, its businesses, its networks.

When SAKALA launches its first Harvest Day in Brockton, the story writes itself. Youth in Haiti grew these goods. Their diaspora in Brockton... the most concentrated Haitian community in the Northeast... picked them up at church on Sunday. The loop closed. The money went from Brockton to Haiti and came back as moringa, castor oil, and spices grown by young people who would otherwise have no job.

That is not a subscription box story. That is a community transformation story. And it starts in a church basement in Brockton, Massachusetts.
